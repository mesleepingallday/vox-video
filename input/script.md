**Chapter 1 — The Copycat From Shenzhen**

In November 1998, five young men in Shenzhen started a small software company. Their leader was Ma Huateng. In English, he goes by Pony, a play on his family name, Ma, which means "horse."

Their first big product wasn't original. It was a copy. An Israeli company had built ICQ, one of the world's first internet chat programs, and America Online had bought it. In February 1999, Tencent released a Chinese-language version and called it OICQ. Open ICQ.

AOL objected. It filed an arbitration claim in the United States, arguing that Tencent's OICQ web addresses infringed the ICQ trademark. So Tencent renamed the product. The new name was just two letters: QQ.

QQ took off. It hit a million users in its first year. But it was free, and every new user cost money in servers and bandwidth. Tencent didn't turn a profit until 2001\.

So picture the company in early 2001\. Growing fast. Burning cash. And looking for someone willing to bet on it.

That someone came from the other side of the world. Naspers was a South African media group that started out in 1915\. In 2001, it paid about 32 million dollars for a 46.5% stake in Tencent.

Hold on to that number. Thirty-two million dollars.

In 2019, Naspers moved its Tencent holding into a new company listed in Amsterdam. By then, CNN reported, the stake was worth about 118 billion euros, or roughly 130 billion dollars. That's around four thousand dollars of value for every dollar Naspers put in, and that's after Naspers had already sold part of the stake along the way.

It became one of the most successful technology investments ever made. And it gave Tencent the fuel for everything that came next.

**Chapter 2 — The \$400 Million Bet**

By the late 2000s, QQ had made Tencent a giant in China. It was also building a games business. And every games business needs the same thing: hits.

One of those hits came from a small studio in Los Angeles called Riot Games. Its game was League of Legends. Tencent was already an early investor in Riot and the game's distributor in China. Then, in February 2011, Tencent went much further. It paid about 400 million dollars for a 93% stake in Riot.

At the time, Riot's CEO Brandon Beck told the trade press that "Riot is going to remain completely independent."

Four years later, in December 2015, Riot mentioned something almost in passing. In a blog post about changes to employee pay, it noted that Tencent had bought the rest of the company. The price was never disclosed. Riot was now wholly owned by Tencent.

That's the Tencent playbook in miniature. Buy in big. Promise independence. Let the studio keep its name, its offices, and its culture. Nothing on the box changes.

But independence has limits. Tencent went on to release Honor of Kings, which became its highest-grossing mobile game. According to reporting by The Motley Fool, Riot considered Honor of Kings a League of Legends clone that could pull players away from its own game. The owner and the studio it owned were competing for the same players.

In June 2012, Tencent made its second big American move. It invested 330 million dollars in Epic Games of Cary, North Carolina, then best known for Gears of War. Founder Tim Sweeney later told Polygon that the deal bought about 48.4% of Epic's outstanding shares, or 40% of the company once employee stock options were counted.

Sweeney kept control. But Variety later reported that the deal fundamentally changed how Epic built and released games. Several of its best-known leaders left, including president Mike Capps and designer Cliff Bleszinski. And the new direction led to the game that changed everything: Fortnite.

By April 2022, Epic was valued at 31.5 billion dollars.

Now do the math. Over the years, Tencent's stake has been diluted to around 28%, according to multiple reports. Even at that size, at Epic's 2022 valuation, it would be worth close to nine billion dollars on paper. The original check was 330 million.

**Chapter 3 — The Quiet Empire**

Then Tencent went shopping in Europe.

In June 2016, it agreed to buy about 84% of Supercell, the Finnish maker of Clash of Clans, mostly from Japan's SoftBank. The deal was valued at roughly 8.6 billion dollars, and it valued Supercell as a whole at about 10.2 billion. The promise was familiar. Supercell's management would keep its operational independence, and the company would stay in Finland.

By then, gaming made up more than half of Tencent's revenue.

The list kept growing. Tencent took stakes in Ubisoft, Paradox, Remedy, Techland, Krafton, and FromSoftware. It owns Funcom, the studio behind Dune: Awakening, and Grinding Gear Games, the studio behind Path of Exile, outright.

Then, in November 2025, came a deal that looked a lot like a rescue. Ubisoft, the French publisher, was under pressure and carrying debt. Tencent put 1.16 billion euros into a new Ubisoft subsidiary built around Assassin's Creed, Far Cry, and Rainbow Six. In return, it got a 26.32% economic interest. Ubisoft kept control. CEO Yves Guillemot said the money "deleverages the Group." In plain English, it helped pay down debt.

Here's what makes all of this so unusual. Tencent rarely puts its own name on the box. Riot is still Riot. Epic is still Epic. Supercell is still Supercell. The empire is built out of other people's brands.

And paying for it all is the machine back home: WeChat, called Weixin in China. In the second quarter of 2026, Tencent reported 1.439 billion monthly users across Weixin and WeChat. Its revenue for those three months was 204.8 billion yuan, up 11% from a year earlier. That's about 2.25 billion yuan coming in every single day.

So that's the rise. A copycat chat app becomes a cash machine, and the cash machine buys a piece of global gaming.

But there was a problem. Two of them, actually. One in Beijing. And one in Washington.

**Chapter 4 — Beijing Bites**

On August 3, 2021, a newspaper affiliated with Xinhua, China's state news agency, published an article about kids and video games. It called online games "spiritual opium." It singled out Tencent's own Honor of Kings, and it cited a student who said some kids played the game for eight hours a day.

In China, the word "opium" carries heavy history. Tencent's stock fell as much as 11%. Later that day, the article reappeared with the phrase "spiritual opium" removed. Tencent's shares still closed down 6.1%.

Within hours, Tencent announced new limits on how long minors could play. It also banned children under 12 from making in-game purchases.

It wasn't enough. At the end of August, regulators set new rules. Anyone under 18 could play online games only from 8 to 9 p.m., and only on Fridays, weekends, and public holidays. For most weeks of the year, that's three hours.

This is the part of the story Western audiences tend to miss. Tencent is enormous. But at home, it doesn't make the rules. And that helps explain why so much of its money kept flowing west. As TechCrunch noted back in 2019, regulatory risk at home is one reason Tencent kept buying stakes in game studios overseas.

But the West was getting less comfortable, too.

**Chapter 5 — Washington Wakes Up**

The first shot came in August 2020\. President Trump signed an executive order targeting WeChat, alongside a similar order for TikTok. The administration said apps developed and owned by Chinese companies threatened national security.

WeChat users in the United States sued. In September 2020, a federal magistrate judge, Laurel Beeler, blocked the ban. She wrote that there was considerable evidence some Chinese technologies posed a real security threat, but "the specific evidence about WeChat is modest." The ban never took effect. In June 2021, President Biden revoked the orders and replaced them with a new review process.

Round one went to Tencent. Round two was quieter, and it hit gaming directly.

On December 18, 2024, the Justice Department announced that two Tencent-nominated directors had left the board of Epic Games. The issue was a 1914 antitrust law, the Clayton Act, which bars the same people from sitting on the boards of competing companies. The department's reasoning was simple. Tencent owns Riot. Riot competes with Epic. So Tencent's people shouldn't sit in Epic's boardroom.

Epic said the two directors resigned voluntarily because of the department's concerns. Tencent also gave up its right to appoint directors to Epic's board on its own. There was no court fight. Under pressure, Tencent simply stepped back.

Then came the bigger blow.

In January 2025, the Pentagon added Tencent to something called the Section 1260H list. It's a roster, required by a 2021 defense law, of companies the Pentagon considers "Chinese military companies" operating in the United States.

Tencent's response was blunt: "Tencent's inclusion on this list is clearly a mistake. We are not a military company or supplier."

Investors weren't so sure. According to Reuters, Tencent's Hong Kong shares fell 7.3% the next day, wiping out about 35.4 billion dollars in market value.

So what does the label actually do? Less than it sounds, at least at first. It isn't a sanctions list, and it doesn't ban Tencent from doing business in America. But the Pentagon can't work with listed companies, and a ban on the Pentagon buying goods and services from them took effect at the end of June 2026\.

Tencent wanted off. In June 2026, the Pentagon published its updated list, now 188 entities long. Tencent was still on it. The Pentagon's document says Tencent is "indirectly affiliated" with the People's Liberation Army. The designation doesn't require the Pentagon to lay out its evidence publicly, and Tencent has been seeking its removal ever since.

**Chapter 6 — The Divestment Question**

Which brings us to March 2026\. The Financial Times reported that senior White House officials had held meetings on a striking question: should Tencent be allowed to keep its stakes in Epic, Riot, and Supercell at all?

The concern, as reported, is data. Officials were weighing whether Tencent's ownership could give it access to information on millions of American players.

Let's be precise about what's known. The reporting we reviewed doesn't describe any finding that Tencent has actually accessed American player data. The question officials were debating is whether it could.

There's a technology angle, too. Citing the FT, Tom's Hardware reported that the U.S. Army has worked directly with Epic for years on early versions of its Unreal Engine technology. Tencent's stake in the company that builds that engine added to the scrutiny.

This review isn't new. According to the same reporting, it sits with CFIUS, a Treasury-led committee that screens foreign investment, and it has dragged on across two administrations without a resolution. Under President Biden, then–Deputy Attorney General Lisa Monaco reportedly pushed for a forced sale. The Treasury Department reportedly preferred letting the investments stay, under data-protection safeguards.

When the FT story broke, Tencent declined to comment, and Tencent's shares fell.

Meanwhile, Tencent has been fighting back the Washington way. In July 2025, John McEntee, who ran presidential personnel in Trump's first term, registered as a lobbyist for Tencent's American arm. According to a disclosure reported by the Washington Examiner, he was paid 175,000 dollars for his work between July and September 2025\.

His argument, as he told Bloomberg in September 2026, is that the designations are "an unnecessary point of tension" in the relationship with China. "Trump's appointees," he said, "should update the lists to reflect their boss's thinking."

So here's where things stand as of early October 2026\. Tencent is still on the Pentagon's list. Public reporting shows no final decision on the divestment review. And when Presidents Trump and Xi met in Washington in late September, that list was one of the irritants hanging over the talks.

**Chapter 7 — What's Really at Stake**

This part is analysis, not reporting.

If Washington ever forced a sale, Riot would be the most obvious target, because Tencent owns it outright. At Epic, Tencent is a minority shareholder with no board seats left. Any forced sale of Riot would need a buyer big enough to afford it and acceptable to regulators. That's a short list.

But cutting the cord would cut both ways. Tencent isn't just an owner. It's a source of money. Ubisoft used Tencent's cash to pay down debt. And the flow runs back the other way, too. In the second quarter of 2026, Tencent said Riot's Valorant hit a record for daily players on PC in China.

That's the irony at the center of this story. An American-made game is helping drive a Chinese company's growth at home. And that Chinese company is helping fund studios in America and Europe.

**Ending — Why It Matters**

So how did Tencent end up with a piece of so much of Western gaming?

Patience. Cash. And a playbook. It bought in early, often when studios needed money. Most of Riot cost 400 million dollars. A huge piece of Epic cost 330 million. It let founders keep their names and their cultures. And it never needed you to know who it was.

That's the deeper lesson about power in the modern economy. The most influential owner isn't always the one with its logo on the screen. Sometimes it's the one on the list of shareholders.

For two decades, that model worked because the world was getting more connected. Now the world is splitting apart. Washington sees ownership as a security question. Beijing sees games as a social question. And Tencent is standing right between them, holding a stake in some of the biggest games on Earth.

Whether it gets to keep them may not be decided by players, or even by markets. It may be decided by two governments.
